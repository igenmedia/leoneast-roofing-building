Roof work sits in an awkward spot in the planning system. Most of it needs no permission whatsoever, which is why homeowners often assume none of it does - and then discover otherwise halfway through a loft conversion. The London Borough of Bromley makes this trickier than average, because it carries three overlapping layers of protection: 47 conservation areas, 13 Areas of Special Residential Character under its own Local Plan, and Article 4 directions that have removed permitted development rights for upward extensions across 16 areas in parts of eleven wards. Roughly one address in five around Orpington and Petts Wood sits under at least one of those. This guide explains which layer does what, which of them touches ordinary roof repair (spoiler: none), and how to check your own address in about two minutes.

## The short answer: repairs no, changes maybe

<img src="/images/blog-planning-permission-roof-bromley.webp" alt="Tiled and slate roofs across a residential conservation area street in Bromley" width="900" height="600" loading="eager" fetchpriority="high" />

The single most useful distinction in all of this is between repair and alteration.

Repair and maintenance needs no planning permission anywhere, under any designation, in any borough. Replacing broken tiles with matching tiles, rebedding a ridge, renewing a lead flashing, clearing and replacing guttering, re-covering a flat roof in the same material - all of that is maintaining what is already there, and the planning system is simply not interested in it. This covers the overwhelming majority of the work we are called out to. If you are having [hip and ridge mortar rebedded on a 1930s semi](/roof-repairs), you can stop reading and ring a roofer.

Alteration is different. Changing the roof covering to a materially different type or appearance, adding a dormer, raising the ridge, adding a storey, or putting in rooflights that project beyond the plane of the roof - these change the building rather than maintain it, and they are where permitted development rights, and the removal of those rights, start to matter.

Nationally, a great deal of roof alteration is permitted development, meaning you can do it without applying. The whole point of the designations below is that they take some of those automatic rights away in particular places.

## Bromley's 47 conservation areas

<img src="/images/blog-bromley-conservation-area-street.webp" alt="A protected street of period houses with original slate roofs and chimneys" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Bromley has 47 conservation areas, which is a lot even for a large outer London borough. You will find them around Chislehurst, in the older core of Farnborough High Street, at Chelsfield village, in Bromley town centre, near the parish church at West Wickham, and in numerous pockets across Beckenham and Keston.

Inside a conservation area, permitted development rights are already narrower than elsewhere. Cladding is out. Side extensions and larger rear extensions lose their automatic rights. Most relevantly for roofs, alterations that would be permitted two streets away can require a full application, and the council will expect materials to match what is already there - the same slate, the same profile of ridge tile, the same colour of clay.

What conservation area status does not do is stop you repairing your roof. Like-for-like repair remains maintenance. Where it bites is on the decision to swap ornamental crested ridge tiles for plain ones, or natural slate for a concrete look-alike, because those change the appearance of a street the designation exists to protect. Details of the borough's designations are published on the council's own [conservation areas pages](https://www.bromley.gov.uk/conservation/conservation-areas-2).

## Areas of Special Residential Character - the Bromley-specific one

<img src="/images/blog-petts-wood-garden-suburb-houses.webp" alt="1930s garden suburb houses with half-timbered gables and hung tiles" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

This is the designation most people have never heard of, and it catches out homeowners in some of the borough's best streets.

Bromley has 13 Areas of Special Residential Character, protected under Policy 44 of its Local Plan. Petts Wood, The Knoll, Park Langley and Bickley are among them. An ASRC is not a conservation area - it is a locally defined designation aimed at the character of a planned residential area rather than at historic fabric. In practice it means development is expected to respect the established pattern of the area and not exceed existing building heights.

Petts Wood is the clearest example on our patch. The estate was laid out in the 1930s by the developer Basil Scruby, largely to designs by the architect Leonard Culliford, as a coherent garden suburb - curved tree-lined avenues, half-timbered gables, steep pitches, and clay tiles hung down the upper walls. That consistency is precisely what the designation protects, and it is why a roof alteration that changes the outline of a Petts Wood house is a planning conversation rather than a builder's decision. Ordinary repair, including refixing a slipped panel of [hung tiling on a gable](/tiling-and-slating), is unaffected.

## Article 4 directions and the upward extension question

<img src="/images/blog-loft-conversion-dormer-roof.webp" alt="A flat-roofed dormer window projecting from a tiled pitched roof" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

In March 2022 Bromley Council made a set of Article 4 directions withdrawing the permitted development rights that allow upward extensions of up to two storeys on certain residential properties. The directions cover 16 areas across parts of eleven wards: Bickley, Bromley Common and Keston, Bromley Town, Chelsfield and Pratts Bottom, Chislehurst, Clock House, Copers Cope, Hayes and Coney Hall, Kelsey and Eden Park, Petts Wood and Knoll, and Shortlands.

An Article 4 direction does not ban anything. What it does is remove the automatic right, so that a full planning application to the council is required for work that would otherwise have gone ahead without one. The practical effect for a homeowner is a delay of a couple of months and an application fee, plus the real possibility of refusal where the proposal would harm the character of the street.

Bromley has separate Article 4 directions in force too, including ones covering roof alterations in particular places and others covering office-to-residential and HMO conversions. The council publishes the full list and an interactive map on its [permitted development and Article 4 pages](https://www.bromley.gov.uk/PermittedDevelopment), which is the authoritative place to check rather than any summary, including this one.

## What if you are in Sidcup, Swanley or Biggin Hill?

<img src="/images/blog-kent-border-village-rooftops.webp" alt="Rooftops of a village on the Kent border with open countryside beyond" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Postcodes and planning boundaries do not line up around here, and getting this wrong wastes an evening at minimum.

**Sidcup is Bexley, not Bromley.** It sits right against the boundary and shares a great deal of its interwar character with the BR postcodes next door, but Bromley's Article 4 directions and its Areas of Special Residential Character have no application there at all. Bexley maintains its own conservation areas and its own directions.

**Swanley is not in London.** A BR8 postcode, a very London feel, and Sevenoaks District Council in Kent for every planning purpose. Different local plan, different conservation list, no Bromley Article 4 - and a genuine Green Belt boundary on three sides, which constrains anything that adds volume far more than it constrains a repair.

**Biggin Hill is Bromley, despite everything.** A TN16 postcode, a thoroughly Kentish setting on the North Downs ridge, and the London Borough of Bromley for planning, building control and conservation. This is the one that surprises people most, and we regularly meet homeowners up there who have spent an evening reading Sevenoaks' guidance by mistake.

## Building regulations are a completely separate thing

<img src="/images/blog-roof-insulation-building-regulations.webp" alt="Insulation being installed between roof rafters during a re-roofing job" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

This is where the most expensive misunderstandings happen, because people who have correctly established that they need no planning permission then assume there is nothing else to satisfy.

Planning permission is about how a building affects its surroundings. Building regulations are about whether it is safe, structurally sound and adequately insulated. They are administered separately, they are triggered separately, and you can very easily need one without the other.

For roofing, the practical rule is that replacing more than a set proportion of a roof covering generally brings the work within building regulations, principally on thermal performance and structure. On a full re-roof that usually means bringing the insulation up to current standards, which is one reason a properly specified quote can look higher than one that has quietly ignored the requirement. The same applies to a flat roof where more than half the area is being renewed. The government's guidance on [when you need planning permission in England and Wales](https://www.gov.uk/planning-permission-england-wales) is a good starting point, and the [Planning Portal's page on roof projects](https://www.planningportal.co.uk/permission/common-projects/roof) sets out the permitted development position for dormers and rooflights specifically.

## How to check your own address in two minutes

<img src="/images/blog-checking-planning-map-online.webp" alt="A person checking a local authority planning map on a laptop" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Three steps, in this order.

First, work out which authority you are in. If your postcode is BR1 to BR7, or TN16 at Biggin Hill, you are almost certainly the London Borough of Bromley. DA14 and DA15 at Sidcup are Bexley. BR8 at Swanley is Sevenoaks District Council in Kent.

Second, check the designations. Bromley publishes an interactive Article 4 map, and its conservation area and ASRC boundaries are on the same site. Put your postcode in and you will know within a minute whether any of the three layers touches you.

Third, and only if the job is an alteration rather than a repair, consider a lawful development certificate. Bromley, like most authorities, will not give informal advice on whether a specific proposal is permitted development, but you can apply for a certificate of lawfulness which gives you a formal, binding answer. It costs less than a planning application and it is a great deal cheaper than building something and being asked to take it down.

One last thing worth saying plainly. If your job is a repair - tiles, mortar, flashings, gutters, a leak traced and stopped - none of the above applies to you, whichever street you live on. We will tell you on the survey if we think your work crosses the line into alteration, and point you at the right page on the right council's website. It is not a question anyone should be answering from a ladder.
