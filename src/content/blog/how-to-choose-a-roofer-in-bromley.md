Hiring a roofer is unusually hard to get right, and the reason is structural rather than moral: it is the only trade where you cannot see the work. You can look at a kitchen, walk on a new floor, run a tap. You cannot inspect a roof, which means you are buying on trust and on paperwork. That is exactly why the roofing trade attracts a certain amount of chancing, and why the difference between two quotes on the same Orpington semi can genuinely be four thousand pounds. This guide sets out six questions worth putting to anyone who prices your job. None of them requires you to know anything about roofing. All of them are questions a firm doing the job properly will answer in a sentence, and a firm cutting corners will find some way around.

## Question one: are you actually local, or is this a call centre?

<img src="/images/blog-choosing-roofer-bromley.webp" alt="A roofer working on the tiled roof of a suburban house in Bromley" width="900" height="600" loading="eager" fetchpriority="high" />

A great many numbers advertised as "emergency roofers Bromley" route to a national booking service, which then subcontracts your job to whoever has capacity. You pay a referral margin on top of the work, you have no relationship with the person who actually turns up, and if something goes wrong in two years' time the entity you contracted with may have very little interest in it.

The test is straightforward. Ask where the person coming out is based. A real local firm answers with a place you recognise - Bromley, Beckenham, Petts Wood, Orpington - and can tell you roughly how long the drive is. If the answer is vague, or is a region rather than a town, you are talking to an intermediary.

Being local matters practically as well as ethically. Most of the BR postcodes are ten to twenty minutes from Bromley, which is the difference between a make-safe happening this afternoon and happening on Thursday. It also means the firm has worked on your kind of house before. Someone who has rebedded a hundred hips across Crofton already knows what is under your ridge tiles. ## Question two: what is the access going to cost?

<img src="/images/blog-scaffolding-cost-bromley-house.webp" alt="Scaffolding erected against the side of a two storey house for roof work" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Scaffolding is frequently the single largest line on a roofing invoice and it is the one homeowners are least prepared for. On a standard two-storey house in this area, allow £800 to £1,600, charged by the hire period. A chimney access scaffold is cheaper. Some repairs are perfectly safe from a tower, which costs a fraction of either.

So ask three things: is access included in the figure you have given me, how long does it stay up, and who pays if bad weather extends the job. A firm that itemises access without being asked has nothing to hide in it. A firm that gives you a single all-in number and gets vague about the breakdown may be planning to bill you separately later.

This is also where you find out whether the quote is based on a real survey. Anyone who has been on your roof knows whether a tower will reach it. Anyone who priced from the driveway does not, and their access figure is a guess that will need revising.

## Question three: can you show me the fault?

<img src="/images/blog-roofer-photographing-fault.webp" alt="A roofer photographing a defective roof detail during a survey" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

This is the question that sorts roofers faster than any other, and it works on every kind of job. A roofer who has found your problem can photograph it. A roofer who is guessing will describe an area rather than a defect - "there's an issue around the chimney" rather than "this cracked cement fillet on the east side, here, is where the water is getting in".

Ask for photographs before you discuss money. Two things follow. First, you can see for yourself whether the proposed work matches the problem. Second, when you get a second quote, you can hold the two descriptions against each other and find out whether the two firms are even talking about the same thing. It is remarkable how often they are not.

On leaks specifically, ask whether they went into the loft. Water travels along rafters and across insulation before it drops, so the entry point can be several metres from the stain on your ceiling. The evidence of the route lives on the underside of the roof. A survey that never opened the loft hatch has skipped the half of the job where leaks are actually solved, which is why so many people end up paying three firms to patch the same ceiling. ## Question four: what exactly are the battens?

<img src="/images/blog-roof-battens-breathable-membrane.webp" alt="New treated roof battens and breathable membrane on a stripped roof" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

This one only applies to re-roofing, and it is the single most reliable way to tell a proper quote from a cheap one. Battens are the horizontal timbers everything else is nailed to. They are the cheapest component in a roof and the one most often downgraded to win a job, because nobody can see them once the tiles are on.

A good quote specifies graded, treated battens to the current British Standard, with a size. A quote that says "new battens" and stops there has left itself room. Untreated softwood battens are a large part of why so many postwar roofs across the Cray valley and out towards Swanley are needing doing again after sixty years.

While you are at it, ask whether the roof is being stripped or overlaid. Overlaying means laying a new covering on top of the old one. It is quicker, it is cheaper, it buries every existing problem underneath, and it adds a great deal of weight to a structure that was never designed to carry two roofs. Any firm offering it as a cost saving is offering you a problem in twenty years' time.

## Question five: what does the guarantee actually cover?

<img src="/images/blog-roofing-guarantee-paperwork.webp" alt="A written guarantee document and job paperwork on a table" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Every van says "all work guaranteed" and it costs nothing to paint on. Ask for the guarantee in writing before you agree to anything, and then read what it covers.

There are two separate things and they get blurred constantly. A workmanship guarantee covers the labour - if the repair fails because of how it was done, the firm comes back at no cost. A manufacturer's warranty covers the materials and runs on the manufacturer's own terms, often for longer. You want both, and you want to know which is which. A firm that explains the difference happily is a firm that has thought about it.

Then ask the harder question: who honours it if you stop trading? There is no perfect answer for a small local business, but an honest one is worth more than a confident one. It is also worth checking whether membership of a trade body offers any backing - the [National Federation of Roofing Contractors](https://www.nfrc.co.uk) sets standards for its members, and [TrustMark](https://www.trustmark.org.uk), the government-endorsed quality scheme, operates a dispute resolution route for registered businesses. Neither is a legal requirement to trade, so treat both as useful signals rather than as a substitute for insurance and a written document.

## Question six: if this is an insurance job, how do you document it?

<img src="/images/blog-storm-damage-insurance-documentation.webp" alt="Wind damaged roof tiles being photographed for an insurance claim" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

If the damage was caused by a storm, the paperwork matters nearly as much as the workmanship, and this is where a great many claims quietly fail. Insurers pay for storm damage and decline damage caused by gradual wear. The difference between those verdicts is usually decided by photographs taken in the first day or two - before anybody climbs up with tools.

So ask: will you photograph the damage before you touch it, and will you describe the failure in writing for the adjuster? A roofer who does this as standard is worth considerably more than the difference in their quote, because a rejected claim costs a great deal more than a call-out.

Be equally alert to the opposite problem. Anyone who urges you to claim before they have properly assessed the damage, or offers to cover your excess, is a firm to walk away from. On a small job a claim can genuinely cost you more over time than paying directly once the excess and the premium increase are counted. This corner of the country does take real storms - the Great Storm of October 1987 gusted to around 130mph and hit west Kent about as hard as anywhere in England, and Storm Eunice repeated a milder version in February 2022 - so the question comes up here more than the rainfall figures suggest.

## What a fair price actually looks like around Bromley

<img src="/images/blog-comparing-roofing-quotes.webp" alt="Two written roofing quotations laid side by side for comparison" width="900" height="600" loading="lazy" />

Numbers help, so here are ours as a benchmark. A single slipped tile refixed is £180 to £320. Rebedding one hip is £350 to £650, and doing all four hips and the ridge on a 1930s semi is £900 to £1,600. A chimney flashing renewed in lead is £450 to £950. Clearing the gutters, which is the best value job in roofing and prevents the most expensive ones, is £90 to £180. A full re-roof on a standard three-bedroom semi runs £7,000 to £14,000 including scaffolding, new treated battens and breathable membrane.

Roughly 40 per cent of the repair work we carry out in the Farnborough and Crofton postcodes is hip or ridge mortar, which tells you something useful: on the interwar streets around here, most quotes should be in the hundreds rather than the thousands. If somebody has looked at a mortar problem and quoted for a re-roof, get a second opinion.

Two final things. Check the firm has public liability insurance and ask to see the certificate - it takes ten seconds and almost nobody does it.
